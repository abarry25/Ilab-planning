Part one

The full picture

Read this part if you have ten minutes. It is the shape of the job, the judgement the job actually requires, the portfolio it carries, the rhythm it runs on, and what the membership community looks like.

1.1What this role actually is

The title reads like event and application administration. The work is not that. Across two years the consistent pattern is converting a program or service into a repeatable operating system — a lifecycle, an ownership model, a calendar, a data flow, an SOP, a feedback loop. Nearly every artifact in the record is infrastructure rather than a one-off.

The clearest articulation of the division of labour comes from the PIC section of the transition document, and it generalises across the portfolio:

The operative distinction

Tom owns strategic oversight, venture-development point of view, and judge/network relationships. Becca contributes evaluation philosophy and ecosystem strategy. Advisors own substantive evaluation and founder preparation. This role owns the operating machine.

Three consequences a successor should absorb early:

  • The role is the connective tissue between Tech, Marketing, Operations and Advising. Almost nothing it owns can be completed without one of those four teams, which is why so much of the documentation is routing tables and RACI splits rather than content.
  • It is also the institutional memory function. Program debriefs, gap maps, and post-mortems were built here deliberately, to move knowledge out of individual heads.
  • Equity of access is an explicit design goal, not a side effect. The Founder Wayfinder exists because a founder's start at the i-lab correlated with their ability to navigate an institution rather than the merit of their idea. That framing recurs throughout the work.

The standing tension, and why it is the actual skill

Everything above describes what the role owns. This is how it decides, and it is the part that does not appear in any job description. Almost nothing here has one correct answer. Most questions arrive with three or four workable options, each of which is better for someone and worse for someone else, and the work is holding two things in view at once:

What the member experiences

The MX frame — see 2.8

Is the entry process clear and the pathway visible? Is content staged so it builds confidence rather than overwhelming? Is progress visible, is recognition built in, is peer connection intentional? Does the environment reinforce founder identity? These are the questions the work is ultimately accountable to.

What the organisation can actually carry

Operations, admin, capacity, compliance

Who staffs it, on which day, at what cost to their deep work. Which system of record has to change. Which team has to say yes. What it does to the inbox, the calendar, the security list. Whether it survives the person who built it leaving. These are the constraints that decide whether the first column ever happens.

Neither column wins by default. A decision that optimises only for member experience produces something nobody can sustain past its first term; a decision that optimises only for operational load produces something technically running that no member values. No decision here has zero impact

Because this role sits between Tech, Marketing, Operations and Advising, a change that looks local almost never is. Moving an intake date moves the security list and the comms calendar. Adding a benefit changes what the front desk has to answer and what the eligibility language has to promise. Simplifying a form changes what the dashboard can report. Deferring a workshop frees a week and empties a member’s month. The second-order effect is usually the expensive one, and it usually lands on a team that was not in the conversation.

Two habits follow from that, and a successor should adopt both early. Name the trade out loud rather than resolving it quietly — much of the documentation in this record exists because a decision was written down with its cost attached, which is what makes it reviewable later by someone who was not there. And ask who else this lands on before deciding, not after. The routing tables, RACI splits and status vocabularies scattered through this dossier are not bureaucracy; they are the accumulated answer to that question, written down so it does not have to be re-asked every time.

The version of this to say out loud in week one

There are many options for most things, and no decision has zero impact on the organisation or on the user. Weigh both, deliberately, every time — and be able to say afterwards what you traded and why.

Judge operations — the part that reliably goes wrong

Tom's notes on the 2026 cycle are the most operationally specific thing in the record, and they describe a sequence that must run in order:

  1. Clear last year's judging assignments — either delete the accounts or strip the old information for returning judges. Tanu owned checking this. Skipping it corrupts the new cycle's assignments.
  2. Expectation-setting email from Mailchimp, sent by Tom after RSVP confirmation: rubric, how to evaluate, the evaluation deadline, when applications appear — and an explicit warning that the next email comes from SMA at a different address.
  3. Upload confirmed judges (exported from Salesforce) into SMA with notify via email selected.
  4. Notify when applications go live in judge accounts, from SMA.
  5. Monitor who has not logged in and nudge them — this is manual and is where no-shows are prevented.
  6. Deadline reminder, then individual extension offers and scoring-exception follow-ups for incomplete or outlying reviews.

The full judge comms arc is specified as thirteen sends: three RSVP reminders, orientation, platform-access readiness, pre-launch follow-up, judging launch, mid-cycle reminder, final deadline reminder, post-deadline extension offer, scoring exception handling, judging close and appreciation, feedback collection, finalist announcement, and event re-engagement.

The 30-day activation problem

The finding the whole redesign rests on

Members who are activated within 30 days of joining — meaning they have attended an event or met with an advisor or expert — are much more likely to stay engaged. Everything in the membership work stream is an answer to a single question that follows from it: how do we engage members within their first 30 days?

That question was put to the whole organisation in a cross-team brainstorm, and the ideas that came back cluster into seven moves. The list is worth keeping intact: it is the closest thing the i-lab has to a shared theory of activation, and it was generated by the people who would have to run it.

Eliminate choice at the entrance

The most repeated idea

New members are overwhelmed by options and never start. Give them a diving board — “here is what you are doing” — and let active choice come once they are acclimated.

Concierge over correspondence

Personalised onboarding

An individual onboarding with the follow-up meeting booked before the first call ends, concrete touchpoints and action items rather than a sequence of emails, and point people or cohort owners where bandwidth allows.

Peer cohorts from day one

Instant community

Peer-based cohort groups during onboarding, themed or common-interest groupings, blind-date style matches between two or three members, and meet-the-founders events with people from their own school.

Low-friction, high-value, fast

Show value before asking for commitment

Early Zoom sessions on broadly useful topics so value is visible immediately; first Founder Fundamentals matched to their stage; more orientations, in more formats — a recorded video is not engaging where a live virtual event is.

Make progress visible and rewarded

Passports, bingo, gamification

What was working

2.5Calendar planning & cross-team design

Section 3.4 covers calendar governance — how an event gets from a draft to a published page. This is the other half: the annual exercise of deciding what the organisation is going to run at all, and getting every team to commit to it in the same place at the same time. It is the least visible thing in the portfolio and the one with the widest blast radius, because every other team’s year is built on top of its output.

The work, in the order it actually happened

  1. Outline every vertical in the organisation. There was no existing list. Producing one — naming each functional area and what it is responsible for programming — was the first deliverable, and it is the artifact everything downstream depends on.
  2. Make sure every group is actually represented. This is the part that cannot be systematised. It ran on relationships and historical knowledge: knowing which teams under-report, which programs run every year but nobody writes down, and which leads need to be asked directly rather than emailed. A planning board is only as good as its least-engaged contributor, and closing that gap was manual work.
  3. Collect the line items. Every row needed communications requirements, specs, and deadlines, gathered from ten-plus event and program leads. Each one is a small negotiation about dates someone else also wants.
  4. Build the drafting tool, because the collection step does not survive being run over email or in a spreadsheet with competing copies.
  5. Export into ClickUp so the plan matches the Operations and Events team calendar, which is the system of record everything else reads from.
  6. Present it back. Several meetings with all stakeholders, including the functional leads, giving a high-level snapshot of what was planned and every moving piece it touched.

Why a tool rather than a spreadsheet

The planning board exists because the hard part of annual planning is not recording dates — it is getting a dozen people to stake out their year against each other, see the collisions while there is still time to move, and end up with one shared version rather than eleven. Everyone viewing the board sees the same grid, and changes save themselves. That single property is what makes the collection step finish.

The planning board

One board per term — Fall 2026 and Spring 2027 each keep their own — with a row for every program or task and a column for every day. Its working parts:

  • Day view and week view. Mark individual days, or collapse to weeks to claim a whole week at once. Collapsing is non-destructive: day-level marks are preserved and come back on expand.
  • Ownership in the row. A small initials box per row, and a notes field for cadence or context — so the board answers “whose is this” without a separate RACI.
  • Direct editing. Click a name to rename it, + Add row at the bottom of a section, hover to reveal × to remove — with a few seconds of undo, because destructive actions in a shared document need a way back.
  • Registrar milestones pinned above the grid, pulled from the FAS Registrar’s published calendar. The board carries its own warning to double-check exact dates before locking programming around them.
  • A draft deadline on the face of it — the Fall board carried Draft due Mon, Aug 4, the same date as the functional-leads review in 3.4. The board and that meeting are one process.

The four group tags — the taxonomy worth keeping

TagWhat it marks

MACROEcosystem events to track — things the i-lab does not run but must plan around

The ClickUp export

Tick any rows, generate the export, and the board produces one line per marked date — editable before download, then out as CSV for import. The column set is built to land cleanly in the Operations calendar rather than to describe the plan:

Task name · Assignee · Start date w/ time · End date w/ time · Preview summary · Description · Format · Location · Zoom link · Audience · List

This is the step that makes the planning board real rather than decorative. A plan that does not reach ClickUp does not reach Central Intake, and an event that does not reach Central Intake does not get published.

The communications planner

A second board in the same tool, built on the first. Channels are lanes, sends are sticky notes on the day they go out, journeys run as solid spans with entry cutoffs, and automations run as hatched spans — live for a window, waiting on a trigger rather than a date.

Its one load-bearing design decision

Content slots are picked from what is actually on the calendar, imported as the Central Intake CSV from ClickUp. A plan therefore cannot quietly promote something that is not running. Every failure this prevents — promoting a cancelled workshop, a send that lands after the event it advertises — is one that normally gets caught by a person reading carefully, or not at all.

Four message types are modelled separately because they behave differently: a dated send goes out on one day; a journey runs over weeks with people joining at different points; an entry email is the welcome belonging to one enrolment window; an automation fires on an action and only while it is switched on. The distinction that matters operationally is the enrolment cutoff — must be in system by — which decides who is eligible for a given message. Leave it empty and everyone in the system gets it.

Mailchimp conditions, not tags — and why

Audiences are built from facets, each reading one Mailchimp field: Founder status reads Member Type. Stacking one value per facet produces combinations such as Returning Members + Founder, and the tool writes the condition Mailchimp needs — Membership Cohort is not Fall 2026 AND Member Type is Founder. Conditions are used rather than tags because a segment re-evaluates itself as people change, while a tag has to be reapplied by hand. This is the reason open decision 11 (confirming exact picklist strings for Member Type and Membership Cohort) matters more than it looks: the whole segmentation model is written against those strings.

Wireframes define an email or Slack post’s block skeleton once and are reused; blocks marked per segment repeat per audience and become the show/hide blocks in the built email, and each send records whether it ships as one email with conditional blocks or as separate sends. A sent link field holds the campaign URL or Slack permalink afterwards, so the plan keeps pointing at the real thing. Download copy sheet exports one row per block, in order, for whoever assembles the send.

A validation bar at the top counts what is in the plan and flags what looks wrong — a Slack post with no channel, an automation with no trigger, a cutoff that excludes everyone, a message promoting an event that will already be over. It is deliberately advice rather than a gate: nothing stops you saving, because sometimes the odd-looking thing is what you meant.

Two things a successor needs to establish

Where this lives, and who keeps it running. The tool is hosted on a free-tier service under an individual’s account. Hosting, ownership and continuity are not recorded anywhere, and neither is whether the board data is backed up. If the year’s plan lives only here, that is a single point of failure worth resolving before the next planning cycle.

The pipeline an event travels

Four teams touch every public event, in a fixed order. The sequence is set out in the Eventbrite process SOP and the status-flow documentation:

  1. Programs drafts the event — title, description, format, audience, date.
  2. Advisors or the Community Manager add substantive detail — what the session actually teaches.
  3. Operations approves logistics — room, setup, catering, AV.

A draft that stalls is almost always stalled at a handoff rather than in someone’s queue, which is why the status vocabulary matters more than it looks.

2.8Learning products — Feedback Lab, Sprints, Primers

This is the newest and most experimental body of work, co-led with Rebekah Emanuel, and it is where the role moved from operating existing programs to designing new ones. Three distinct products share one architecture: teach first, ask last, with an editable content layer kept separate from the delivery logic.

The four-part model everything is tested against

Before any individual product, there is a shared frame. Learning design at the i-lab is decomposed into four parts, and each pilot is an experiment on some subset of them:

01 · Content

What we believe founders should learn

Learning arcs, topics, frameworks, tools; the split between core and optional. Tested as content alignment — is this actually helpful for making progress?

02 · Delivery

How learning is connected and facilitated

Pedagogy, learning conditions, resource overlap and layering, contextual application. Tested as user behaviour — are users taking meaningful actions that indicate progress?

03 · Container

Where learning happens

Digital and physical experiences, UX/UI, marketing. Tested as usability — can users quickly figure out what to do and how to do it?

04 · Entry

Getting people into the container

Data, intake, eligibility, admin. Notably, Entry is the one quadrant the Feedback Lab pilot did not test — participants arrived through PIC, so intake was solved for it.

The same four parts, asked as feelings

A parallel Member Experience frame asks each quadrant an experiential question rather than an operational one. Entry: is the process clear, is the pathway visible, how does it feel, where does belonging happen? Content: is it staged appropriately, does it build confidence, does it avoid overwhelm? Delivery: is psychological safety embedded, is recognition built in, are milestones celebrated, is peer connection intentional? Container: can founders navigate easily, is progress visible, does the environment reinforce founder identity? These are the questions the redesign is ultimately accountable to.

Why the Feedback Lab was worth building

The pilot began from an observation about waste. Online judging produces thousands of reads across 150+ venture applications and returns 1,000+ lines of highly specific expert feedback, which was then mail-merged to 150+ semifinalists and left there. The feedback was objectively helpful, there was a great deal of it, some founders loved it, and some did not love receiving criticism — and individually it was overwhelming and very technical. The question that started the work was simply whether clear trends existed underneath it. They did: the four themes above.

Three assumptions are being tested and should be stated as assumptions, because the pilot exists to check them: that non-finalist teams are too early to implement some of this feedback; that these founders are highly engaged and want to make progress; and that the feedback is clear, accessible, and provides a clear next step.

What it is really a pilot for

Four strategic payoffs are named, and they explain why a small email experiment got senior attention. Member experience — potential pilots for alumni and affiliates. Resource allocation — leveraging work already being done to test something new. Content reflection — reformatting existing content and defining its learning power. Validation — debunking or confirming assumptions about user behaviour and needs.

Five hard constraints — each one is a real failure mode

  1. Never use a contact tag in a conditional block. Conditional merge tags read fields and groups only. A tag holds no value, so it cannot be printed.
  2. Never put conditional logic in a subject line. It is not supported — and this is the single deciding factor for whether something is one email or separate sends.
  3. Never segment or personalise on an unsynced field. It is empty for every contact, so the segment selects nobody and fails silently. Currently empty: Feedback - Improvement, Feedback - Strengths, Ingenuity Award Idea, Sign up, lastname.

  5. Never assume a picklist value. Confirm the exact string in Salesforce first: Founder and Student Founder are different audiences.

The word “tag” means three unrelated things in Mailchimp — a merge field's placeholder, a contact label, and (loosely) a segment. The SOP exists mostly to keep those apart. The core tension

It is not a disagreement about who we value. It is that the language we use to talk about eligibility does not match the systems we use to verify it.

  • MIDAS speaks registrar. The verification system returns enrollment classifications and institutional status, not the categories the policy is written in.
  • Thirteen schools, thirteen vocabularies, no single source of truth. “Research Associate” means a senior postdoc at FAS and an ongoing staff appointment at HBS. Both readings are correct at their own school.
  • Title is not a reliable signal. Confirmed directly by the FAS Director of Postdoctoral Affairs: postdocs are not students, staff or faculty; departments name them differently; income comes from Harvard, other institutions, governments and stipends.
  • The best available signal is not available. Job codes would settle most cases — FAS has confirmed which classes are postdocs — but job codes are not in MIDAS. Access to them was still being pursued when this record closes.
  • Postdocs and clinical fellows are often classified as employees, not enrolled students, which is exactly the population funding rules are most likely to care about.

The consequence is stated precisely in the February 2026 discussion: our policy language is broader than what our verification infrastructure can consistently validate. That produces confusion during funding review, inconsistent decisions, staff burden, and risk exposure if a funding decision is ever challenged.

What opening the door fully would cost

Many people at the i-lab believe membership should simply be open to everyone. It is a reasonable position and it removes an enormous amount of work. Two costs come with it, and they are worth stating in full because they are the arguments that do not get made in the room.

Psychological safety

The one that is hardest to recover

When membership was students only, we could promise every member that the space was made up of students pursuing entrepreneurship. That promise is what makes people willing to be early, unpolished and honest in public. Open it to later-career people under the same word “member” and two things follow: competitions skew toward the more advanced, and a vulnerable community becomes reachable by people with predatory intent — investors and angels among them.

This argument carries less weight internally than it deserves. It is recorded here because it is the cost that does not show up in any operational metric until after it has been paid.

Member experience

The one that arrives immediately

If everyone is eligible for membership, the gates have to move somewhere — onto the resources. That means higher, more manual verification at every individual gate rather than one check at the door. And it means people are told they are members and then discover they cannot use the things membership appeared to include.

The record describes the result directly: members believing they are eligible for PIC or for perks and discounts, finding out they are not, and producing disappointment, anger, and a great deal of back-and-forth customer service. A permissive front door buys a hostile interior.

Why this is a leadership decision and not an operations one

Three stakes are named in the record, and none of them can be settled by whoever is doing the checking. Harvard ecosystem relationships and reputation. Compliance with the i-lab charter and with Harvard IP and equity rules. And the credibility of the i-lab as an IP-free, equity-free, investment-free educational institution — which is the identity the psychological-safety argument is ultimately protecting.

How it actually runs day to day

  • MIDAS checks are a scarce resource. Access is granted by HUIT and capped at roughly three to five people. In practice two or three team members plus the Assistant Director of Student Programs run the checks, and anything complex is marked discussion required and escalated to the AD.
  • The register is an Airtable. Fifty-nine programme and status rows, each carrying a membership decision, a PIC decision, a funding decision and a space-access decision. It is the closest thing to a rulebook, and it is a working document rather than a policy.
  • AY26 changed the model. Eligibility checks were piloted only for funding opportunities — PIC, SIFF, HiPo. Membership now relies on attestation after SSO login. This is the single largest operational decision in this section: it moved the cost of verification from the front door to the point where money is involved.
  • Extension School is the exception that stayed. DCE confirms degree-candidate status. The contact became a reviewer inside the application system with weekly digests, and ineligible applicants receive specific language directing them to the HES Career and Academic Resource Center rather than a generic decline.
  • Graduation has its own machinery. The school’s representative determines whether that person is eligible for student benefits. Pushes the judgement to the institution that owns the vocabulary — which is the DCE model, generalised.

“The honor code”

We define eligibility across the i-lab ourselves. Applicants who do not clear MIDAS or SSO are asked to disclose more about their status, and a decision tree determines eligibility from their answers. Keeps the judgement in-house and accepts self-report as the input.

They trade the same thing in opposite directions: who carries the interpretive burden, and therefore who carries the risk when an interpretation is wrong.

Two tiers, which is the structure underneath all of it

Tier 1 and Tier 2

Tier 1 is the first layer — does this person have access to student benefits at all? Tier 2 is everything specific to an individual offering, including rules that have nothing to do with identity, such as not having won PIC funding within the past two years. Separating them is what makes the AY26 model work: Tier 1 can be attestation at the door, because Tier 2 is where the real check happens.

The framework for deciding, when someone is ready to decide

A full policy architecture exists and is the most structurally complete piece of thinking in the record. It moves in four stages, each answering a different question:

Four risk categories

Reputational

Risk to program credibility, fairness perception, or institutional trust.

Compliance

Risk of violating institutional policy, donor intent, grant restrictions, tax rules, or audit standards.

Operational

Risk that the policy creates inefficiency, inconsistency, staff burden, or decision instability.

Mission

Risk that eligibility standards undermine the program’s core purpose.

The Tech–Programs relationship is part of the fix
